\section{Proximity Structures and Metrics} \label{chap-ps-metrics}
To be able to compare two stimuli pairs in a mathematical sense, we introduce a relation on a stimuli set that compares two stimuli pairs.
If we have four stimuli $\ps{a}, \ps{b}, \ps{c}$ and $\ps{d}$, then $(\ps{a},\ps{b})\succsim(\ps{c},\ps{d})$ denotes that, the stimuli $\ps{a}$ and $\ps{b}$ are equal or less similar than the stimuli $\ps{c}$ and $\ps{d}$ (or more precisely, the dissimilarity between $\ps{a}$ and $\ps{b}$ is at least as great as that between $\ps{c}$ and $\ps{d}$). Analogously, $\sim$ means that the similarity of two pairs is equal.
This essence of a \emph{proximity structure} is exactly that, with some additional constraints.

\begin{defi}[Proximity Structure] \index{Proximity structure} \label{def-prox-struct} %\gls{PS}
	Suppose $\ps{A}$ is a set and $\succsim$ a quaternary relation on $\ps{A}$, i.e., binary relation on $\ps{A} \times \ps{A}$. Then $\langle \ps{A}, \succsim \rangle$ is a \emph{proximity structure} (PS) iff (if and only if) the following axioms hold for all $\ps{a}, \ps{b} \in \ps{A}$:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\succsim$ is a weak ordering on $\ps{A} \times \ps{A}$, i.e., it is connected and transitive.
		\item $(\ps{a}, \ps{b}) \succsim (\ps{a}, \ps{a})$ whenever $\ps{a} \neq \ps{b}$.
		\item $(\ps{a}, \ps{a}) \sim (\ps{b}, \ps{b})$ (minimality).
		\item $(\ps{a}, \ps{b}) \sim (\ps{b}, \ps{a})$ (symmetry).
	\end{enumerate}
	The structure is \emph{factorial} iff \index{Proximity structure!factorial} \label{def-fact-prox-struct}
	$$
		\ps{A} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \ps{A}_i.
	$$
\end{defi}

\begin{rem*}[Connectedness]
	Connected means, that comparison of all distinct elements is possible, i.e., for a relation $R$ on a set $X$ it holds for all $x, y \in X$ with $x \neq y$ that either $xRy$ or $yRx$.
\end{rem*}

We briefly explain these axioms.
The first axiom is the ordering assumption, which serves as the basis for proximity measurement.
Axiom 2 imposes a natural constraint: other-dissimilarity must exceed self-dissimilarity.
Axiom 3 requires self-dissimilarity to be equivalent across all objects, and axiom 4 require dissimilarity to be symmetric.
In the following chapters, we will introduce additional axioms that extend the concept of proximity structures. In the final chapter, we will discuss these axioms in more detail, particularly regarding their empirical validity.
The mathematical concept we aim to connect proximity structures to is the concept of a metric. A metric is defined as follows:

\begin{defi}[Metric] \index{Metric} \index{Metric space} \label{def-metric} %\gls{metric} \gls{metricSpace}
	Let $X$ be a set. A real-valued function $\metric$ on $X \times X$ is a \emph{metric}, iff the following properties hold for all $x, y, z \in X$:
	\begin{enumerate}[1.]
		\item $\metr{x}{y} > 0$ unless $x=y$ and $\metr{x}{x} = 0$
		\item $\metr{x}{y} = \metr{y}{x}$
		\item $\metr{x}{y} + \metr{y}{z} \geq \metr{x}{z}$
	\end{enumerate}
	We call $\langle X, \metric \rangle$ a \emph{metric space}.
\end{defi}

Metrics are measures of distance in a space. The third property is called the \emph{triangle inequality} and is central to the intuition of measuring distances. The triangle inequality can be formulated as follows: Adding a detour through $y$ on the way from $x$ to $z$ can only increase the distance.

Since we aim to connect these two concepts, we will introduce a term for that which aligns with the intuitive approach: We want the metric to preserve the structure of $\langle A, \succsim \rangle$. The only structure we have is the ordering of two stimuli pairs, and we want the metric to preserve exactly this ordering.

\begin{defi}[Representability of $\succsim$ by a Metric] \index{Proximity structure!representable by a metric} \label{def-representable-by-metric}
	A proximity structure $\langle \ps{A}, \succsim \rangle$ is \emph{representable by a metric} iff there exists a real-valued function $\delta$ on $\ps{A}$ such that:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\langle \ps{A}, \delta \rangle$ is a metric space, i.e., $\delta$ is a metric on $\ps{A}$.
		\item $\delta$ preserves the relation $\succsim$, i.e., for all $\ps{a}, \ps{b}, \ps{c}, \ps{d} \in \ps{A}$
		      $$
			      \delta(\ps{a}, \ps{b}) \geq \delta(\ps{c}, \ps{d}) \text{ iff } (\ps{a}, \ps{b}) \succsim (\ps{c}, \ps{d}).
		      $$
	\end{enumerate}
\end{defi}
\textcite[p. 162]{foundations2} prove that representability by a metric does not impose any empirical constraints.
However, simple metrics do not provide enough structure, and we therefore require the metrics to satisfy additional conditions, which we can observe in often used metrics.
One of the most used metrics is the Minkowski $p$-metric or Minkowski distance (also called the power or $L^p$ metric) and is defined in the following way.

\begin{defi}[Minkowski Metric] \index{Metric!Minkowski} \index{Metric!power} \index{Minkowski!metric} \index{Minkowski!distance} \label{def-minkowski-metric} %\gls{minkMetric}
	For $n \in \mathbb{N}_{>0}$ and $p \geq 1$ the \emph{Minkowski Metric} of two vectors $\ve{x}, \ve{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is defined as
	\begin{equation*}
		\metr[p]{\ve{x}}{\ve{y}} = \left ( \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|^p \right)^{1/p}.
	\end{equation*}
\end{defi}

For $p \geq 1$, this is indeed a metric, as the Minkowski inequality establishes the triangle inequality \parencite[pp. 189-191 Theorem 9]{handbookMeans}. \index{Minkowski!inequality}

If we closely inspect this metric, we can observe two interesting properties.
The first one is, comes directly from the definition of the metric. For two vectors we compute the metric by doing four steps.
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
	\item Decompose two vectors $\ve{x}$ and $\ve{y}$ into their underlying coordinates $(x_1, \dots x_n)$ and $(y_1, \dots, y_n)$.
	\item Calculate the coordinatewise differences $|x_i - y_i|$ and transform then with a bijection $F(x)$ (in this case $x^p$).
	\item Sum up the transformed differences for all coordinates.
	\item Transform the sum using the inverse $F^{-1}$ (in this case $x^{1/p}$).
\end{enumerate}
This generalization of the metric yields the \emph{Additive-Difference Model} (\cref{def-add-diff-model}) which will be covered in \cref{chap-AD-model-and-ps}.

The second thing we can observe in the Minkowski Metric is the fact that distances across straight lines are additive (we will show this in \cref{ex-minkowski-seg-additive}). This is not the case for all metrics, but only for a special class of metrics. This class of metrics, called \emph{metric with additive segments}, will be studied in \cref{chap-seg-additivity}.

In \cref{chap-AD-model-and-ps} and \cref{chap-seg-additivity}, we will inspect the constraints that each representation imposes on the proximity structure and deduce necessary axioms. These axioms then lead to new structures which allow such representations. This is shown by the representation and uniqueness theorems which are presented at the end of both chapter. We do not prove these theorems, for that refer to \textcite[Chapter 14]{foundations2}.

Instead, we will focus on combining both representations. This leads to the main result: the Unique Representation Theorem for Segmentally Additive AD Models (\cref{thm-uniquene-repr-minkowski}), which states that the Minkowski metric is the only additive difference metric that is also segmentally additive.
